Turmeric vs Curcumin: Absorption, Black Pepper and Full Spectrum Extracts Explained

Turmeric and curcumin are not the same thing, though you would never know it from the labels. Understanding the difference explains why so many supplements need black pepper, fats or concentrates added, and why ours does not.

Written by Benita & Phil, Three Springs Farm · 8 minute read

The short of it

  • Curcumin is about 5% of turmeric. It is one part of the plant, not another name for it.
  • On its own, curcumin is cleared by the liver quickly, so it absorbs poorly.
  • Black pepper, added fats and curcumin concentrates all exist to patch that problem.
  • Piperine's effect is unpredictable, and it switches off a liver process you want working.
  • Whole turmeric arrives with its own oils already in place, so the problem never begins.

Curcumin is not another name for turmeric

Turmeric is one of nature's most complex healing plants. Beneath its golden colour lies a living network of active compounds, working together in quiet harmony. This is how turmeric has supported human health for thousands of years. Whole. Balanced. Intact.

Curcumin is one part of that network. It makes up about 5% of the rhizome. It can be separated out from the plant, and it can also be manufactured synthetically from non-plant material.

Somewhere along the way, the words 'curcumin' and 'turmeric' became interchangeable in the media and on supplement labels. They are not. Turmeric holds more than 100 active compounds, and curcumin, while the most studied of them, is far from the only one doing useful work.

Put simply: curcumin is a component of turmeric, in the way that caffeine is a component of coffee. A curcumin product and a turmeric product are not the same thing, and they do not behave the same way in the body.

Among those other compounds is a group of natural oils called turmerones. Hold on to that word. It turns out to matter a great deal to everything that follows.

Why is curcumin poorly absorbed?

Isolated curcumin has a well-documented problem: the body does not hold on to it. It is rapidly metabolised and excreted by a liver-induced process, which limits how long it stays available to absorb.

That single fact explains most of what you see on supplement labels today. Build a product around isolated curcumin and you inherit its absorption problem, which then has to be solved with something else in the bottle. That something else is usually black pepper, added fats, or a curcumin concentrate.

What black pepper actually does

Black pepper releases a natural oil called piperine. Piperine slows the liver process described above, which keeps curcumin in circulation for longer and lifts its bioavailability.

The mechanism is real. But it comes with a catch that rarely makes it onto the packaging: black pepper only assists curcumin when curcumin is isolated on its own. Add it to a whole turmeric extract and there is nothing left for it to fix, because the plant already brought its own support along.

Why there is conjecture over black pepper

Two practical concerns, both worth knowing.

The timing is unpredictable. Piperine has to be released at the same moment enough curcumin is present in the digestive system. Those two conditions do not reliably line up, so the absorption benefit varies from dose to dose.

Switching off a liver process has a cost. The same pathway that clears curcumin also helps your body handle toxins, pollutants and medications. Temporarily inhibiting it may compromise that defence. Black pepper also has a tendency to irritate the stomach.

None of this makes black pepper a villain. It is a wonderful thing on your dinner. The question is whether adding piperine to a supplement is the best way to solve an absorption problem that a full spectrum extract does not have in the first place.

What is a curcumin concentrate?

A curcumin concentrate is a patented product, either extracted from natural turmeric or synthesised from non-plant material. Concentrates are used to lift curcumin to a standardised level in capsules, tablets or liquids, so every batch can declare the same number on the label.

An identical number is commercially useful. It is not the same thing as a product that reflects how the compounds actually occur in the plant.

How fats and oils aid absorption

Fats and oils act as carriers for curcumin and extend the time it stays in circulation. This is why some formulations add oils, and why the old advice to take turmeric with food has always been sound.

Here is the part we find quietly remarkable. Whole turmeric already contains its own natural oils that do exactly this job. The turmerones stripped out when curcumin is isolated are the same class of compounds that formulators then add back in another form.

So why are these things added at all?

They are added to offset the low absorption of curcumin by the human body. That is the whole reason.

Each additive is a patch for a problem created upstream, at the moment curcumin was separated from the plant that came with its own support system. An extract made from the whole fresh rhizome never has to solve that problem, because it never creates it.

No synthetics. No isolates. No artificial enhancers.

Standardised vs full spectrum extracts

This is the distinction sitting underneath everything above.

 Standardised extractFull spectrum extract
What it is Active compounds adjusted to an identical proportion in every batch, typically above naturally occurring levels Made from the whole plant or plant part, containing all compounds in the same proportions as provided by nature
Consistency Identical declared curcumin figure batch to batch Natural variation, shaped by each harvest
Usual additives Often black pepper, fats or curcumin concentrates to offset absorption None required. Turmeric's own turmerones do the work
Typical raw material Commonly dried powder or syrups Fresh whole rhizome

Neither approach is dishonest. They are simply answering different questions. A standardised extract asks how to guarantee an identical curcumin figure in every capsule. A full spectrum extract asks how to deliver the plant as it grew.

We have always been more interested in the second question.
